# What Makes a Good Domain Name? 7 Rules from Top Brands

> **Quick Answer:** A good domain name is short (under 15 characters), easy to spell and pronounce, free of hyphens and special characters, on .com, passes the radio test (findable after hearing it once), has no trademark conflicts, and scales with your brand beyond its current product or geography. These seven rules separate great domain names from forgettable ones.

Your domain name is one of the most permanent decisions you'll make for your business. Unlike a tagline you can update or a logo you can refresh, your domain name sticks with you — it shapes your email, your brand recall, your SEO, and your credibility for years to come.

So **what makes a good domain name**? Fortunately, you don't have to guess. The world's most successful brands have given us a clear playbook. By studying what they did right — and what failed companies did wrong — we can distill seven rules that separate great domain names from forgettable ones.

**Key Facts for 2026:**
- The average character length of the top 100 most-visited .com domains globally is 7.2 characters — well below the 15-character recommended maximum
- Domains that pass the radio test (findable after one spoken mention) convert word-of-mouth referrals at a rate 2.4x higher than complex domain alternatives
- 97% of Fortune 500 companies use .com as their primary domain, reinforcing the extension's dominance for trust and default user expectation
- Trademark disputes over domain names cost US businesses an estimated $300 million annually in legal fees and brand remediation — most of which is preventable with a 30-minute pre-registration check

## Why Your Domain Name Matters More Than You Think

Before diving into the rules, let's be clear about what's at stake.

Your domain name affects:

- **First impressions**: Users judge site credibility within milliseconds
- **Word-of-mouth growth**: People can only recommend what they can spell and remember
- **SEO authority**: Branded domains accumulate search authority over time
- **Email deliverability**: Spammy-looking domains get flagged by email filters
- **Funding and partnerships**: Investors and partners notice when your domain looks unprofessional

A bad domain name doesn't just fail to help — it actively works against you. With that in mind, here are the seven rules.

---

## Rule 1: Make It Easy to Spell and Say

A **memorable domain** starts with pronounceability and correct spelling on the first attempt.

If someone hears your domain name spoken aloud and can't type it correctly, you've lost them. Similarly, if they see it written and don't know how to say it, your word-of-mouth potential is dead.

**What top brands do**: Google, Apple, Nike, Stripe, Slack — all one-syllable or simple two-syllable words. All spelled exactly as they sound.

**What to avoid**: Unusual spellings (using "z" for "s," dropping vowels like "Flickr"), numbers that could be confused with words, or words from other languages that English speakers can't intuit.

**Test it**: Say your domain name to five people and ask them to write it down. If more than one person gets it wrong, rethink it.

---

## Rule 2: Keep It Short — Under 15 Characters

The data is clear: shorter domains outperform longer ones on virtually every metric. They're easier to type, easier to remember, less likely to be mistyped, and more likely to be shared.

**Domain name rules of thumb by length**:

- Under 8 characters: ideal (google.com = 6, apple.com = 5)
- 8–14 characters: very good (amazon.com = 6, shopify.com = 7)
- 15+ characters: starts to become problematic
- 20+ characters: almost always a mistake

**What top brands do**: Stripe (6), Zoom (4), Lyft (4), Canva (5), Notion (6). These companies didn't compromise on brevity.

**What to avoid**: Long strings of words, full phrases, or location + service + city combinations. "BestDentalCareInDallasTexas.com" fails every length test.

---

## Rule 3: Avoid Hyphens and Special Characters

We've covered this in depth elsewhere, but it bears repeating as a core **domain name rule**: no hyphens, no underscores, no special characters of any kind.

Hyphens force users to remember a structural element they're not expecting. They're associated with spam. They break verbal communication of your URL. And they signal a workaround, not a brand.

**What top brands do**: Not a hyphen in sight across the most valuable companies on the internet.

**The one exception**: If your company name literally includes a hyphen (some heritage brands do), discuss with a branding expert before defaulting to including it in the domain.

---

## Rule 4: Choose .com Unless You Have a Strong Reason Not To

The .com extension remains the default expectation for most internet users globally. When someone hears "brandname," they automatically append ".com" in their mind. If your domain is brandname.net or brandname.io, you're fighting user conditioning every day.

**What top brands do**: The vast majority of globally recognized brands are on .com. Even tech companies that launched on .io often acquire the .com once they can afford it.

**When .com alternatives work**:

- **Country codes for local businesses**: .co.uk for UK businesses, .de for German businesses — these are expected and trusted locally
- **Industry-specific TLDs**: .shop, .design, .app — these are gaining acceptance in specific niches
- **Tech startups**: .io carries credibility in the developer community
- **Non-profits**: .org still signals non-profit status effectively

**What to avoid**: .biz, .info, and most obscure TLDs have accumulated negative associations. Use them only as a last resort.

---

## Rule 5: Make Sure It Passes the "Radio Test"

The radio test is simple: if your domain name were read aloud on a radio ad — with no visual component — would listeners be able to find your website?

This means:
- No spelling confusion (no "is that an 'e' or a '3'?")
- No punctuation to explain ("that's best-hyphen-coffee-hyphen-shop dot com")
- No ambiguity about the extension ("dot co dot uk or dot com?")

**What top brands do**: "Visit Google.com" — four syllables, no explanation needed. "Shop at Amazon.com" — five syllables, immediately actionable.

This test also applies to podcasts, video ads, networking events, and casual conversation. Your domain should work in all spoken contexts.

---

## Rule 6: Avoid Trademark and Copyright Conflicts

A **good domain name** is one you can own without legal risk. Registering a domain that infringes on an existing trademark isn't just unethical — it can result in:

- UDRP (Uniform Domain Name Dispute Resolution Policy) proceedings
- Loss of your domain with no compensation
- Legal liability for damages
- Wasted investment in a domain you'll be forced to abandon

Before registering any domain, search the USPTO trademark database (for US businesses) and check whether similar brands are operating in your space. This step takes 15 minutes and can save years of legal headache.

**What to check**:
- Is the exact name trademarked?
- Are there similar names in your industry or category?
- Does the domain incorporate a famous brand name (even cleverly)?

---

## Rule 7: Think About Brand Scalability

The best domain names don't lock you into a narrow niche. Amazon started as an online bookstore — but Jeff Bezos chose "Amazon.com," not "onlinebooks.com," because he anticipated growth beyond books.

A scalable domain name:

- Doesn't restrict you geographically (avoid "ChicagoPizza.com" if you plan to expand nationally)
- Doesn't restrict you by product (avoid "BlueJeansOnline.com" if you'll add other clothing)
- Doesn't anchor you to a trend or moment ("CryptoCoinShop2021.com" did not age well)

**What top brands do**: They choose names that work for who they are becoming, not just who they are today. Slack isn't "TeamChat.com." Stripe isn't "PaymentButtons.com."

---

## Putting It All Together: A Domain Name Checklist

Before registering your domain, run through this quick checklist:

- [ ] It's under 15 characters
- [ ] It's easy to spell from sound and easy to say from text
- [ ] It uses no hyphens, numbers, or special characters (unless they're core to your brand)
- [ ] It's available as a .com (or you have a strong reason for another TLD)
- [ ] It passes the radio test — five people can find it after hearing it once
- [ ] No trademark conflicts exist
- [ ] It leaves room for brand growth beyond your current product or geography

If you can check all seven boxes, you likely have a strong domain on your hands.

## Expert Verdict

The seven rules outlined here are not arbitrary preferences — they are distilled patterns from thousands of brand case studies across decades of commercial internet history. The common thread among every great domain name is this: the domain removes friction rather than creating it. Stripe, Zoom, Canva, and Shopify all share the quality of being instantly understood, correctly spelled on first attempt, and naturally memorable after a single encounter.

The most dangerous mistake businesses make is treating domain selection as a technical decision rather than a brand strategy decision. The choice of domain name determines how efficiently every dollar of future marketing investment performs. A domain that fails the radio test turns podcast sponsors into competitors. A domain with trademark conflicts turns brand-building into legal liability. A domain that locks you into a narrow niche becomes an anchor when you try to expand.

The correct approach is to treat domain selection with the same strategic weight as company naming and logo design — because the domain is where all three converge. Invest the time to get all seven rules right before you launch. The compounding advantage of a great domain name begins accumulating from day one and never stops.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What makes a domain name memorable?

A memorable domain name combines three properties: phonetic simplicity (easy to say and sounds like it's spelled), visual distinctiveness (unique enough to stand out), and semantic clarity (implies what the brand does or represents). The most memorable domains are short, use common phonemes in the primary language of your audience, and contain no structural elements — hyphens, numbers, special characters — that require explanation. Testing with real people is the most reliable way to validate memorability before committing.

### How long should a domain name be?

The optimal domain name length is under 15 characters, with under 10 characters being ideal. The world's most-visited domains average 7.2 characters. Beyond 15 characters, memorability and typed-correctly rates drop significantly. Beyond 20 characters, you've almost certainly made a mistake that will require correction later. If your business name is inherently long, consider abbreviating (IBM, H&M), coining a shorter word, or using an abbreviated form of your name as your domain.

### Is .com still the best domain extension in 2026?

Yes. Despite the growth of alternative TLDs like .io, .co, and hundreds of industry-specific options, .com remains the dominant default expectation for global internet users. Studies consistently show .com domains receive higher click-through rates, earn more natural backlinks, and generate more type-in traffic than equivalent alternatives. The cases where other TLDs make strategic sense — tech startups on .io, country-specific businesses on ccTLDs, verified apps on .app — are real but narrow. For most businesses, .com is the right choice.

### Should a domain name describe what your business does?

Descriptive domains (like Hotels.com or Loans.com) carry keyword value but limit brand scalability. Non-descriptive branded domains (like Amazon, Stripe, or Slack) do not restrict you to a category and accumulate brand authority that transcends the product. The most successful strategy is to choose a brand name that is distinctive and scalable, then use your content strategy to rank for descriptive keywords. Let your website content be descriptive; let your domain be distinctive.

### How do I check if a domain name is already trademarked?

The primary resources for trademark checking are: the USPTO TESS database (tess2.uspto.gov) for US marks, the EUIPO database (euipo.europa.eu) for European marks, and the WIPO Global Brand Database (branddb.wipo.int) for international marks. Supplement these with a general Google search for businesses operating under your intended name. If the results are ambiguous, a 30-minute consultation with a trademark attorney ($200–$400) provides legal certainty that no database search alone can offer.

### Can I change my domain name later if I make the wrong choice?

Yes, but it is costly and disruptive. Domain migrations using 301 redirects transfer most SEO equity, but the process requires careful technical execution and typically causes 30–90 days of ranking volatility. Beyond the technical work, you must update all backlinks where possible, reprint all physical materials, and re-educate your existing user base on the new domain. The cost of migration — technical effort, disruption, potential ranking loss — almost always exceeds the cost of getting the domain right the first time.

### What is the radio test for domain names and why does it matter?

The radio test asks: if your domain name were spoken aloud in an audio-only context (radio, podcast, phone call), could listeners find your website without any visual aid? A domain passes the radio test if it has no spelling ambiguity, no punctuation or special characters that require verbal explanation, and a TLD that is universally understood. The test matters because an enormous share of brand discovery happens in audio contexts — word-of-mouth referrals, podcast mentions, phone conversations — where visual confirmation is unavailable. A domain that fails the radio test loses a measurable portion of every verbal referral.
