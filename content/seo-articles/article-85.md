# How Domain Escrow Works (And Why You Should Always Use It)

> **Quick Answer:** Domain escrow works by having a neutral third party (typically Escrow.com) hold the buyer's payment until the seller transfers the domain and the buyer confirms receipt. Neither party controls both the money and the domain simultaneously, eliminating the risk of fraud on either side. It costs 3.25% or less of the transaction value.

You've found a buyer for your domain. Or maybe you're the one buying a premium name for your business. Either way, you're about to transfer a significant asset and a meaningful amount of money between two strangers on the internet. How do you make sure neither party gets burned?

The answer is domain escrow. Understanding how domain escrow works is one of the most important things any domain buyer or seller can learn — because skipping it is how people lose thousands of dollars in fraud.

**Key Facts for 2026:**
- Domain fraud losses are estimated at tens of millions of dollars annually globally, with the majority occurring in private transactions conducted without escrow protection
- Escrow.com has processed over $5 billion in transactions since its founding and is the officially recommended escrow service by ICANN for domain transactions
- On a $10,000 domain sale, Escrow.com's fee is approximately $175 — less than 2% of the transaction value and a fraction of the loss exposure from an unprotected transaction
- Marketplace-integrated escrow (through Afternic, Sedo, or Dan.com) automatically protects buyers and sellers without any additional setup, making marketplace sales inherently safer than private deals

## What Is Domain Escrow?

Escrow is a financial arrangement where a neutral third party holds funds during a transaction until both parties fulfill their obligations. In domain name transactions, escrow works like this:

1. The buyer sends payment to the escrow service (not to the seller)
2. The seller transfers the domain to the buyer
3. The buyer confirms receipt of the domain
4. The escrow service releases the funds to the seller

At no point does either party control both the money and the domain simultaneously. This structure eliminates the biggest risk in private domain transactions: someone taking the money (or the domain) and disappearing.

## Why Domain Transactions Are High-Risk Without Escrow

Domain transfers happen digitally, between people who often don't know each other, for amounts that can range from $500 to $500,000. This combination creates an environment ripe for fraud.

### Common Domain Transaction Scams

**The "Transfer First" Request**: A fake buyer pressures you to transfer the domain first, promising to pay immediately after. Once you transfer, they vanish.

**Fake Payment Proof**: A scammer sends you a forged PayPal screenshot or bank transfer confirmation. The domain leaves your account; the money never arrives.

**Chargeback Fraud**: A buyer pays via credit card or PayPal, receives the domain, then files a chargeback claiming they never received it. PayPal sides with the buyer and you lose both the domain and the money.

**Overpayment Scams**: A buyer sends a check for more than the asking price and asks for a refund of the difference. The check bounces later. You've sent real money back; they sent nothing.

Domain escrow eliminates all of these risks because funds are verified before the transfer happens, and they're held by a regulated third party.

## How Escrow.com Domain Transactions Work (Step by Step)

Escrow.com is the most widely used and trusted escrow service for domain name transactions. Here's exactly how the process flows.

### Step 1: Both Parties Agree to Use Escrow

The buyer and seller agree to the domain name, price, and currency. Either party can initiate the escrow transaction on Escrow.com.

### Step 2: Create the Transaction

One party (usually the seller) logs into Escrow.com and creates a new transaction. You'll enter:
- Domain name
- Agreed sale price
- Inspection period (typically 5 days)
- Who pays the escrow fee (buyer, seller, or split)

### Step 3: Buyer Funds the Escrow Account

Escrow.com notifies the buyer, who then deposits the agreed amount into the escrow account. Accepted payment methods include wire transfer, credit card, PayPal, and check. Escrow.com verifies the funds before any domain movement begins.

### Step 4: Seller Transfers the Domain

Once Escrow.com confirms the funds are secured, the seller initiates the domain transfer to the buyer. This typically involves pushing the domain (if both parties use the same registrar) or initiating a standard domain transfer with an EPP/authorization code.

### Step 5: Buyer Inspects and Accepts

The buyer has the agreed inspection period (usually 5 business days) to verify the domain has transferred correctly and functions as expected. They log into Escrow.com and click "Accept."

If something is wrong — the domain didn't transfer, or it's not what was described — the buyer can dispute the transaction during the inspection period, and Escrow.com mediates.

### Step 6: Funds Released to Seller

Once the buyer accepts, Escrow.com releases the funds to the seller (minus the escrow fee). The transaction is complete.

## Alternatives to Escrow.com

While Escrow.com is the gold standard, there are a few other options for safe domain transfers.

### Marketplace-Integrated Escrow

Many domain marketplaces (Afternic, Sedo, Dan.com) have their own built-in escrow systems. When a sale completes through one of these platforms, the escrow process is handled automatically. You don't need a separate Escrow.com account.

This is one of the reasons listing on a marketplace is generally safer than selling privately — the protection is built in.

### Flippa Escrow

The Flippa marketplace, which handles domain and website sales, uses Escrow.com as its integrated payment solution. Transactions initiated through Flippa flow through Escrow.com automatically.

### PayPal (Not Recommended)

PayPal is sometimes used for small domain transactions, but it carries serious risk for sellers. PayPal's buyer protection policies make it easy for fraudulent buyers to file chargebacks even after receiving a domain. Avoid PayPal for domain transactions whenever possible.

## When Is Escrow Especially Critical?

Escrow is always a good idea, but it's absolutely non-negotiable in these situations:

**High-value domains**: Any domain above $1,000 should use escrow. The cost is minimal compared to the protection.

**Private sales**: Transactions outside established marketplaces have no built-in protection mechanism.

**Unfamiliar buyers or sellers**: If you don't personally know and trust the other party, escrow protects everyone.

**International transactions**: Cross-border payments have additional fraud risk and slower recourse options.

**Domains with existing traffic or revenue**: The domain's value is higher and verification is more complex — escrow's inspection period gives you time to confirm everything transferred correctly.

## Red Flags That Should Trigger Escrow Insistence

If any of the following happen during a domain negotiation, insist on escrow or walk away:

- The buyer or seller resists using escrow ("it's not necessary, I'm legit")
- They suggest an escrow service you've never heard of and can't verify
- They want to pay via wire transfer directly to your personal account
- They ask you to transfer the domain before payment clears
- They're in an unusual rush and pressuring you to skip steps

Legitimate buyers and sellers have no reason to avoid escrow. Anyone who does is either a scammer or has done enough transactions to know they're asking you to take a risk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Escrow.com safe to use for domain transactions?
Escrow.com is the most trusted escrow service for domain transactions, licensed as a money transmitter, and recommended by ICANN. It has processed over $5 billion in transactions. The service verifies buyer funds before any domain transfer begins, protects sellers against chargebacks, and mediates disputes during the inspection period. It is the industry standard for safe domain transactions.

### Who pays the escrow fee — buyer or seller?
The escrow fee is negotiated between buyer and seller as part of the deal. Common arrangements include the buyer pays, the seller pays, or both split it 50/50. Neither arrangement is standard — it's whatever both parties agree to. Many marketplace transactions (through Sedo or Afternic) absorb the escrow cost within the platform commission.

### Can I use PayPal for a domain sale instead of escrow?
PayPal is strongly discouraged for domain transactions above $100. PayPal's buyer protection policies allow buyers to file chargebacks claiming "item not received" or "not as described" — and domains, as digital goods, are difficult to defend in PayPal disputes. Sellers routinely lose the domain and the money in fraudulent PayPal domain transactions. Use Escrow.com instead.

### What happens if the buyer doesn't accept the domain during the inspection period?
If the buyer does not accept the domain and does not dispute the transaction during the inspection period, Escrow.com automatically releases the funds to the seller after the period expires. If the buyer disputes, Escrow.com reviews the evidence from both parties and mediates the dispute. Sellers are protected from arbitrary refusal to accept a legitimate transfer.

### Do domain marketplaces like Sedo and Afternic have their own escrow?
Yes. Sedo, Afternic, Dan.com, and most major domain marketplaces include built-in escrow processes that automatically protect both parties. When a sale completes through a marketplace, the platform handles payment verification, domain transfer monitoring, and fund release without requiring a separate Escrow.com account. This is one of the primary advantages of selling through a marketplace rather than privately.

### How long does an escrow domain transaction take?
A typical Escrow.com domain transaction takes 5–10 business days from initiation to fund release. This includes the buyer funding escrow (1–3 days depending on payment method), the domain transfer (1–3 days), and the buyer's inspection period (5 business days, though buyers often accept faster). Wire transfer funding is the fastest payment method for initiating the transaction.
