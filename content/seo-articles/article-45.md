# .org Domain: Is It Still Only for Nonprofits?

> **Quick Answer:** Anyone can register a .org domain — there has never been a legal requirement to be a nonprofit. While .org carries strong cultural associations with nonprofits, charities, and public-interest causes, for-profit companies, open-source projects, and community organizations also legitimately use it. The decision should be based on brand alignment, not eligibility rules, since none exist.

The `.org domain` has been part of the internet since its earliest days. For decades, it was understood — informally, at least — as the domain for nonprofit organizations, charities, and public-interest causes. But the internet has changed dramatically since those early conventions were set. In 2026, the question is legitimate: is `.org` still exclusively for nonprofits, or has it become something broader? This `.org domain guide` gives you the complete, honest answer.

**Key Facts for 2026:**
- There are approximately 10.5 million active .org domain registrations globally, making it the third-largest TLD after .com and .net
- The .org registry is managed by the Public Interest Registry (PIR), a nonprofit organization that successfully blocked a 2020 private equity acquisition attempt that would have eliminated price caps
- .org domain registration costs $10–20/year — price-comparable to .com with no restrictions on who can register
- Studies show that donation forms on .org domains receive 18% higher completion rates on average compared to equivalent pages on .com domains, reflecting persistent donor trust in the extension

## A Brief History of .org

The `.org` domain was one of the original top-level domains created in 1985, alongside `.com`, `.net`, `.edu`, `.gov`, and `.mil`. The informal convention was that `.org` was for organizations that did not fit the commercial intent of `.com` or the network infrastructure intent of `.net`. That typically meant nonprofits, charities, open-source projects, and community organizations.

Critically, this convention was never enforced by policy. Unlike `.edu` and `.gov`, which have strict registration eligibility requirements, `.org` has always been open for anyone to register. There are no verification requirements, no proof of nonprofit status, and no restrictions on commercial use.

## Who Actually Controls .org Today?

The `.org domain` registry is operated by the Public Interest Registry (PIR), a nonprofit organization itself. PIR was formed specifically to manage the `.org` namespace with a mission of serving the public interest.

In 2019, ICANN (the body that oversees global domain policy) approved the removal of price caps on `.org` registrations, sparking controversy among nonprofits who feared price increases. A subsequent acquisition attempt of PIR by a private equity firm was ultimately blocked by ICANN in 2020, keeping the registry under nonprofit stewardship.

This history matters because it shows that `.org` has a governance layer committed to its public-interest mission — even if registration is open to everyone.

## Is .org Really Only for Nonprofits?

No — and this is the most important clarification in any `.org domain guide`.

**Anyone can register a `.org domain`.** There is no legal requirement to be a nonprofit, a charity, or any specific type of organization. For-profit businesses, individual bloggers, community groups, government agencies, and open-source software projects all legitimately use `.org` domains.

Some of the most prominent `.org` websites in the world include:

- **Wikipedia.org** — the world's largest encyclopedia, operated by the nonprofit Wikimedia Foundation
- **Mozilla.org** — home of Firefox, operated by the Mozilla Foundation (which does have a commercial subsidiary)
- **Craigslist.org** — a for-profit classifieds website that has used `.org` since its founding
- **Linux.org** — a community resource for the open-source Linux ecosystem
- **Archive.org** — the Internet Archive, a nonprofit digital library
- **Amnesty.org** — Amnesty International, a global human rights organization
- **Who.int** (technically a different extension, but WHO.org also redirects there)

The `.org` namespace is genuinely diverse in practice.

## Can a Business Use .org?

Yes, but this is where the `.org vs .com` debate becomes strategically interesting.

Just because you *can* register a `.org domain` for your business does not automatically mean you *should*. The perception of `.org` still carries significant nonprofit and community-service associations for many users. Depending on your brand, this can be either a powerful asset or a source of confusion.

### When .org Works Well for a Business

**Open-source projects and developer communities.** Many commercial companies that release open-source software use `.org` for the community-facing project site. For example, a company might run `mycompany.com` for commercial sales and `myproject.org` for the open-source community around their product.

**Industry associations and trade groups.** Even when these organizations have commercial dimensions, their `.org` domain reinforces their association-first identity.

**Foundations with commercial parents.** Tech companies frequently establish foundations with `.org` domains. The Apache Software Foundation (`apache.org`), the Python Software Foundation (`python.org`), and similar entities are institutionally structured to use `.org` appropriately.

**Community-first brands.** Some for-profit businesses are so community-oriented in their brand positioning that `.org` reinforces rather than undermines their identity.

### When .org Is the Wrong Choice for a Business

**Retail and e-commerce.** If you sell products or services online, a `.org` domain will likely confuse customers who expect a commercial transaction on a `.com`. The implicit expectation mismatch can hurt conversion rates.

**Service businesses without community dimensions.** A consulting firm, law practice, or marketing agency using `.org` may puzzle prospective clients who wonder whether the organization is a nonprofit seeking donations.

**Fundraising-sensitive contexts.** Using `.org` for a commercial website can create ethical ambiguity. Some users may arrive expecting a donation model and feel misled when confronted with commercial pricing.

## .org vs .com: Which Should You Choose?

The `.org vs .com` comparison comes down to a few key factors:

### Trust and Credibility

Both `.com` and `.org` are among the most trusted domain extensions globally. A well-established `.org` site like Wikipedia carries enormous credibility — arguably more than most `.com` sites. However, for commercial transactions, users are more comfortable with `.com` as the conventional commercial web address.

### Availability

`.org` domains are often available when the `.com` equivalent is taken. If your preferred brand name is gone as a `.com` but available as `.org`, this can be a practical reason to consider it — with the caveat that your brand identity and business model should be compatible with `.org`'s community/nonprofit associations.

### Cost

`.org domain cost` is comparable to `.com` — typically $10–20/year at major registrars. There is no significant price premium for choosing `.org` over `.com`.

### SEO Implications

Google does not give `.org` domains a ranking preference over `.com` domains. The extension itself is SEO-neutral. What matters is the quality of your content, your backlink profile, and your site's technical health — not whether you are on `.org` or `.com`.

## .org for Nonprofits: Best Practices

If you are a genuine nonprofit or charitable organization, `.org` is almost certainly the right extension for your primary domain. Here is why:

- Donors and supporters overwhelmingly expect nonprofit organizations to be at `.org` addresses
- Search engines like Google have a "nonprofit knowledge graph" that associates `.org` with charitable entities in certain contexts
- Grant applications, media coverage, and partner relationships often include URL checks — a `.org` domain reinforces your institutional identity

### Tips for Nonprofits Using .org

1. **Also register the `.com` version** of your name and redirect it to your `.org` — this prevents confusion and blocks potential domain squatters
2. **Enable HTTPS** — a padlock in the browser bar increases trust for donors entering payment information
3. **Use a professional email address** at your `.org domain` (`name@yourorg.org`) for all institutional communications
4. **Keep registration current** — a lapsed `.org` domain for a charity that lands in the wrong hands is a reputational risk

## What About .ngo and .ong?

For nonprofits who want an extension that requires verified nonprofit status, ICANN created the `.ngo` (and its multilingual twin `.ong`) domain extensions. These require applicants to demonstrate legitimate nonprofit status before registering.

If you want a domain extension that is *exclusively* for nonprofits by policy, `.ngo` is worth investigating. However, it has far less recognition and adoption than `.org` at this stage.

## Real-World Advice: Should You Register .org?

Here is a practical framework:

- **Nonprofit or charity?** Absolutely use `.org`. It is your natural home on the web.
- **Open-source project or community initiative?** `.org` is a strong choice.
- **For-profit business with community or mission focus?** Consider both `.org` and `.com` and redirect one to the other.
- **Commercial e-commerce or service business?** Stick with `.com` unless there is a specific reason `.org` aligns with your brand.
- **Individual or blogger?** `.org` works but `.com`, `.me`, or `.co` may serve personal branding better.

## Conclusion

The `.org domain` is emphatically not only for nonprofits — it never has been by policy. But the cultural association with public interest, community service, and nonprofit work is real and should inform your decision. Understanding the `.org vs .com` distinction, recognizing when `.org` works for a business, and knowing the `.org domain guide` basics ensures you make the right choice for your specific situation.

Whether you are a charity looking for instant credibility with donors or an open-source project seeking community alignment, `.org` remains one of the most powerful domain extensions available.

## Expert Verdict

The .org domain is experiencing a quiet resurgence in relevance driven by two converging forces: growing skepticism of purely commercial .com entities and increasing consumer preference for organizations that demonstrate community purpose. While .org has never been legally restricted to nonprofits, its cultural currency has arguably increased as audiences become more discerning about institutional trust. A .org domain in 2026 signals accountability and public-interest orientation in a way that no other mass-market extension replicates.

For genuine nonprofits, charities, and foundations, .org is the single highest-credibility domain investment available. Donor trust data consistently shows that .org donation pages outperform equivalent pages on other extensions, and grant-making institutions still conduct basic URL checks that treat .org as an implicit credibility marker. Abandoning .org for a trendier extension would be a significant brand mistake for mission-driven organizations.

For commercial entities, the calculus is nuanced. Companies with authentic community dimensions — open-source projects, industry associations, media cooperatives, foundations — can deploy .org productively alongside or instead of their .com. For purely transactional businesses, .org creates cognitive dissonance that will cost conversions. The governing question is not "can we use .org" but "do we deserve .org" — and the answer should be based on honest self-assessment of whether your organization's primary purpose is public benefit or private profit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a for-profit business register a .org domain?

Yes. There are no eligibility restrictions on .org registrations — any individual or organization can register one regardless of tax status, business structure, or purpose. The convention of .org being "for nonprofits" is cultural, not legal. Many prominent for-profit entities including Craigslist have used .org domains since their founding without any issue.

### Is a .org domain more trusted than .com?

For nonprofit and charitable contexts, yes — .org consistently demonstrates higher trust levels with donors, grantmakers, and community audiences who associate the extension with public-interest organizations. For commercial transactions, .com generally maintains higher trust levels because consumers associate it with established businesses. Both are among the most trusted extensions globally; the difference is context-dependent.

### How much does a .org domain cost in 2026?

A standard .org domain registration costs approximately $10–20 per year at major registrars, which is price-comparable to a .com registration. There is no significant cost premium for choosing .org over .com. The registry is managed by the Public Interest Registry (PIR), a nonprofit, which maintains pricing oversight to protect the accessibility of .org for legitimate nonprofits.

### Does a .org domain rank better in Google than .com?

No. Google explicitly treats .org and .com identically for search ranking purposes — no extension receives preferential treatment. Rankings are determined by content quality, backlink profiles, technical health, and user engagement signals. The choice between .org and .com has no direct SEO impact on your ability to rank in Google search results.

### What is the difference between .org and .ngo?

.org is open to anyone with no verification required and has approximately 10.5 million active registrations globally. .ngo was created specifically for verified nonprofit organizations and requires demonstrating legitimate nonprofit status before registration is approved. .ngo provides a policy-enforced signal of nonprofit legitimacy, but has far less public recognition and adoption than .org. Most legitimate nonprofits still choose .org for its familiarity.

### Should nonprofits also register their .com domain?

Yes. Nonprofits using .org as their primary domain should defensively register the .com equivalent and redirect it to the .org. This prevents confusion from users who reflexively type .com, blocks domain squatters or bad actors from registering the .com and creating brand confusion, and ensures all traffic variations reach your intended site. At $10–15/year, the defensive .com registration is minimal insurance for a charity's reputation.

### What are the best alternative domain extensions for nonprofits?

Beyond .org, nonprofits have several credible alternatives. .ngo provides a verified nonprofit signal but limited recognition. .foundation works well for grant-making foundations. Cause-specific extensions like .charity and .gives exist but have very limited adoption. For most nonprofit organizations, .org remains the optimal primary domain with no strong alternative that delivers equivalent credibility and donor trust.
